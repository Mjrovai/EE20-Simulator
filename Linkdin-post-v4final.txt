A couple of years ago, I heard Massimo Banzi, the founder of Arduino, talk about how he fell in love with electronics as a child in Italy while playing with a kit. It was the Lectron: little magnetic blocks you snapped onto a metal plate to build a circuit, designed in Germany in the mid-sixties. "I learned electronics with this kit when I was 7," Banzi said.

That took me straight back to my own.

Christmas, 1970. I was 11 and had spent two years looking at that box in a shop window in my Brazilian hometown (Osasco, in São Paulo), and that year my parents finally had the money. Not Santa Claus. My parents.

The box was an "Engenheiro Eletrônico Philips", the EE 20. Inside: a brown pegboard, a bag of springs, two rolls of wire, one AF 116 germanium transistor, two AC 126s, a ferrite rod, a lamp, and a book with 22 circuits to build. Banzi had magnets. I had springs. Neither of us needed a soldering iron, which is probably why our parents said yes.

Going back to that book, I noticed things that passed me by as a kid.

Philips designed the EE series in Eindhoven in the early sixties and kept it going into the eighties. The first kits existed to sell Philips components to hobbyists. By the end of the decade the purpose had shifted: make teenagers curious enough to study it.

My Brazilian edition was printed in São Paulo in 1968, two years before it reached me. The back cover lists a Philips branch in Guanabara, a state that ceased to exist in 1975. The text still carries the accents the 1971 spelling reform removed: rêde, sôbre, êste. The last line reads "Conte com PHILIPS para aprender melhor" ("Count on PHILIPS to learn better").

The circuits were serious. A three-transistor receiver with a ferrite rod you rotated to null out a station. A Morse trainer. A burglar alarm. An eight-key electronic organ you tuned by ear on the potentiometer. Half the book was theory, and it never talked down to the kid reading it.

So I rebuilt it, with help from technology 60 years younger. All 22 circuits now run in a browser.

The simulator has the mounting board with parts you click to inspect, the blue Philips schematic that lights up as the circuit runs, the instruction book, and the parts list with resistor color codes. The sound is real: the organ, the Morse, stations drifting in and out of tune, the howl when the mic nears the loudspeaker. The batteries run down. A fault-finding mode hides a reversed transistor or a swapped resistor for you to track down, straight out of the book's chapter on localização de defeitos.

The code was generated by Claude Fable 5.1 (Anthropic) from the two scanned manuals, under my direction. A 1966 kit rebuilt by a 2026 model, from the book I read as a boy.

Free, in English and Portuguese, runs offline. Both original manuals are there as PDFs, shared for historical and educational purposes.
